The SG Design System: Context

Design systems reflect the companies they're built for. The SG Design System covers over 500 products, focused on SG Markets, the bank's platform of financial applications. It's used by around 1000 developers and multiple design teams worldwide.

The system has to cover everything from marketing pages to dense trading apps (see images). It also covers all of the wildly different technical setups that projects have.

I've led the system since 2019, delivering hundreds of releases, four major migrations and complete asset libraries while keeping funding and pushing the system forward to create user and business value with a small team of five main contributors.

Generations

Over 12 years the system has been through several major generations, each with their challenges. I joined SG to join in the transition from v3 to v4. This was a major redesign and my main role was building UI kits from scratch, first in Adobe XD and then in Figma.

To justify the next evolution I leveraged business needs for more complex components and colour issues to have a more major overhaul. We built a whole new v5 of the system and began the 2 year migration plan, with new advanced web components and design patterns.

Today we're working on the 6th generation — a more complete rebuild with AI at the core. Read more about that in 0/1 here.

The numbers

Adoption is probably the most important factor for a design system. At SG, we got the system mandated at the SG Markets platform level, and every client-facing application must be reviewed and updated to use it.

Often design system complexity comes from multi-brand or platform constraints, but for the SG system the sheer variety of interfaces meant that density (component sizes) and tech flexibility mattered most.

Some of my personal stats: three component libraries built from scratch (two solo), 10+ non-standard components designed and built, hundreds of design reviews and about half of my time spent actually designing products.

Although I don't put much store in figures like the number of variants or Figma usage, it was impressive to see that over two million table cell components were inserted this year.

Strategy

Senior leadership recognises the Design System as a business asset which can cascade decisions and harmonise digital experiences.

Small, sustainable teams have always been my go-to. The SG design system working group has never had more than five members, relying on fast decision-making, expert contributors and regular reviews of setup/organisation. I'm proud that we've survived multiple funding cut threats (ask me about the most recent) and continued to deliver recognised value.

I believe that everyone on the design system team should also work directly on product teams. It avoids system silos and enables better collaboration and visibility.

A lot of the work that we've enabled has been via a foot-in-the-door strategy — folding system updates into strategically important work.

On tooling and tech, I've found it best to stay as up to date as possible on the design side, and cater to whatever engineering teams decide that they want. The SG system has been tech-agnostic intentionally to serve all the different stacks, but now that teams are converging on React, we have a strong opportunity to evolve and focus.

Organisation

I've written extensively about organising the system so I'll direct you to this article first.

We settled on a hybrid governance system with a Design System Council at the core with each member nominated as a main contributor. Designers must work on projects too and we occasionally rotate members in and out of the council. On the development side, as specific front-end expertise is needed, rotation happens rarely.

The council also guides and manages contribution from the active community of users (technical and design). Often I leverage users filing bug reports to tempt them to contribute; otherwise I actively seek out people with expert domain knowledge to help work on specific components. We've had over 35 contributors from the community.

Another effective mechanism is a single entry point for help, feedback and bugs. For more engagement I also write slightly infamous notes for our fortnightly releases. I've found that people actually do read notes — if they are amusing!

Build

I'm still designing and building components. As the system is so mature, there are rarely major updates to the existing components, but with new business cases taking priority and the arrival of AI, there's now plenty to build.

Now that I've built three libraries I consider myself a Figma expert. I also now contribute code to our front-end libraries, increasingly with the help of AI.

Components are rigorously tested in beta with projects that volunteer to be early adopters. Accessibility is ensured and documentation, design tooling and all libraries are then updated, and release notes written, before each release.

Design lab

Design lab is an award winning design/code tool that I designed and help build internally, and scaled to 2,000 users. The tool is simple in that it just gives you an editor to write HTML and then a live preview of your code with the SG Design System applied. I created it first as a tool for the design system team to test new components, but it quickly became the place for sharing templates and snippets, especially after I built features like the component editor and inspect tools.

This kind of tooling has helped break down barriers for non-developers to start coding. Most of the CIB design team now use Design lab instead of making specifications.

The secondary effect was how much I learnt from being a product owner — from budget justifications to product lifecycle and compliance.

Value

The system's value is compounding over time. Initiatives like business patterns (design patterns linked to operational business flows) have been found to cover up to 80% reusability, while the knowledge store is now enabling AI skills to be built which has almost removed any barriers to entry for people wanting to build (more on this in 0/1).

I'll finish with some reflections. First, there's not much I'd change about our actual evolution, as I'm convinced that we mostly took the best paths for the system at the time. I don't regret not adopting design tokens; our solution was as effective and took a tenth of the time.

We should have worked out how to quickly share design patterns and templates; even a basic gallery would have been great. Documentation is always great to fall back on, but active examples are far more effective (albeit time consuming).

Design system tutorial sessions were hugely effective. UI drift is inevitable and needs constant care, especially with flexible systems. Design systems are a utility and a good marker of success is consumers treating them as one.
